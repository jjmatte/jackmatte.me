## To learn, or not learn, with AI

The first time I used an LLM to help me in an actual work task, I was trying to decipher some VBA code written over a decade ago in our database that was housed within Microsoft Access. My boss had mentioned that a developer position was available but that one of his employees would have to prove their capabilities before that role would be theirs. With a growing number of requests from within the company for changes or bug fixes to our forms and reports within the application, we needed someone who could functionally do the work. I took this as an opportunity to grow and jumped in.

I had very little problem learning almost any of the systems at work, as my experience with low voltage systems (fire alarms, instrumentation, etc.) as an electrician coupled with my many years of experience playing around with computers in my free time really allowed me to have a level of expertise my resume would not have reflected. I figured that this would be no exception, and within a few hours I was nursing a headache, having absolutely no idea where to even begin in this house of cards that had been slowly built over years by multiple people. At some point frustration and a desire for production overcame my hesitancy about relying on an agent, and I ran my problem through an agent. Within 15 minutes I had a clear direction, and in another couple of hours I had solved my first development problem.

I then spent about 3 months handling various issues within our Access program, to varying degrees of success, using LLMs to supplement my lack of syntax knowledge as well as the Access application itself. I didn't realize it at the time, but I was wading into a way of coding that would dramatically change the way I work in many ways. At around this time we hired a senior developer for the company to migrate our entire application out of Microsoft Access and into an internally developed web application so that we would no longer be reliant on Microsoft Access for continued management of our database. With this began the first time in my life I was officially employed to develop software.

### Off the deep end...

One of the first conversations I had with my new leader was about whether or not I should continue to study syntax and practice coding without AI in a world where, moving forward, I had access to it. He encouraged me to continue devoting a lot of my time to learning to write, as well as actually writing, my own code. Conversely, at the same time I was reading and watching quite a few opinions on the fact that syntax isn't nearly as important as learning how to create pseudocode. It was definitely something I have spent quite a bit of time considering. What is the best way to learn when you have a tool that can give you the answers?

I am still learning and in no way have figured out the most efficient way to do so. As I have grown as a developer, there have been times when deadlines aren't pressing that I have spent time hand writing code and learning how exacting syntax can be at points. At other times I have used agents to increase production to meet deadlines or resolve high priority fixes as quickly as possible. I think ultimately something in the middle is probably the best approach. I have found that using an agent allows me to learn bigger picture concepts much more quickly than if I had to spend weeks hand typing all the code (or copy pasting from Stack Overflow). That being said, if you don't take the time to read what your agent is producing, that is an opportunity to learn missed. Evaluation and quality control aren't just important for your work quality, they are also incredible tools to learn how syntax matters and what decisions are being made behind the code choices.

Agents are incredibly helpful and shift the required skill set of a developer away from becoming an expert in syntax and more towards being able to evaluate code as well as translate user feedback into useful tools. The more experience I earn working in this new age of coding, the more I rely on my communication skills with my team as well as our clients. Having a priority on proper communication has dramatically increased not only the efficiency but also the quality of the systems we are deploying. Ultimately, that, in my opinion, is the most important part of the job. As far as syntax is concerned, I will just continue to keep growing and learning from as many sources as possible.
